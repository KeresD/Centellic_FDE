# Portfolio and Fund management
- info from SOAS

## ILO:
- how to manage investments -> individual investor and as a professional fund manager (e.g., pension fund, mutual fund or hedge fund)

## Background:
- Deregulation and better tech made financial markets much bigger and created new ways to trade.

- Changes helped caused 2007-2009 financial crisis -> worst since 1930s

- Contributing factors included: 
    - complicated new securities
    - heavy borrowing to fund trades (leverage)
    - little transparency
    - relaxed attitude to risk from investors, banks, and regulators

Can only get higher returns by accepting more risk.

    - Reward = measures as expected return on investment
    - Risk = measures by how much returns jump around (variance or standard deviation)
    - good investment decision depends on your goals, how long you plan to invest, and how much risk you can stomach

Theory assumes markets = efficient --> prices already reflect all available information, so studying past prices shouldn't help beat market.
- looks at when this concept breaks down using behavioural finance, and asks whether mispricing or chart-based trading can actually make money.

## What you invest in, who helps, and where trading happens

1. Financial instrucments, risk and return
- Building blocks of a portfolio - from low risk to high risk:
    - money market instruments (Treasury bills, certificates of deposit)
    - bonds (government and corporate, with risk depending on issuer's credit rating)
    - equity (shares that give part-ownership of a company)
    - derivatives (usually high risk)
- properties and commodities = hard to sell qquickly but can help diversify portfolio

2. Financial intermediates and investment companies
- These are the middlemen who move money from savers to borrowers
    - advisors, brokers, wealth managers, and investment banks
- Pooled investment options like unit trusts, mutual funds, ETFs and hedge funds, plus pension funds

3. Stock markets and benchmarks
- Primary market = where new securities = sold for the first time, through IPOs and seasoned equity offerings
- Secondary market = existing securities = traded, including over-the-counter markets and electronic networks
- Electronic trading can enable algorithmic and high-frequency trading, covers buying on margin and short selling
    - surveys major stock exchanges in the US, Europe, and East Asia
    - also explain how stock indexes = used as benchmarks

### Cover the theory and practices of managing a portfolio

4. Optimal portfolio selection
- Markowitz portfolio theory from 1950s = spreading money across many securities many securities gives a better risk-reward baalnce than putting it all in one. 
- Key insights = best mix of risky assets = same for everyone.
- Can adjust your overall risk by blending that mix with risk-free assets
- Whether returns really follow normal distribution or not?

5. Behavioural finance
- unit challenges assumption that investors = fully rational. 
- draws on psychology to explain how people from beliefs and preferences
- also covers limits to arbitrage: in theory, traders fix mispricings by trading against them but in practice they can't always do so so mispricings can last a long time.

6. Technical Analysis
- Predicitng prices from patterns in past price and volume data, using tools like moving averages and volatility indicators
- algorithmic and high-frequency trading - whether any of it actually works

7. Passive vs active management
    1. Passive - set fixed % targets for broad assset classes and stick to them
    2. Active - change those weightings, pick mispriced securities using fundamental analysis, and time the market
Full Markowitz optimisation = costly because it requires estimating many numbers, so we can opt for: 
    1. Treynot-Black Model - relates each stock's returns to a market index, which cuts down estimation needed.
    2. Black-Litterman model - lets manager adjust standard CAPM (Capital Asset Pricing Model) predictions with their own views
        - CAPM predicts that an asset's expected return is a linear function of its systemic risk, quantifies by its beta relative to the market

            Core predicitions:
            - Expected return scales directly with beta.
            - No Idiosyncratic Reward: Unsystematic risk yields zero extra return.
            - Baseline Compensation: Assets start at the risk-free rate (\[R_{f}\]).
            - Systematic Scaling: Risk premium increases by beta times the market risk premium.

            The Maths:
            - Equation: \(E(R_i) = R_f + \beta_i (E(R_m) - R_f)\)
            - Undervalued Assets: Plot above the SML (higher return for same risk).
            - Overvalued Assets: Plot below the SML (lower return for same risk).
            - Fairly Valued: Plot on the SML line. 

            Real-World Limitations:
            - Investors cannot borrow unlimited funds at \[R_{f}\].
            - Empirical Flaws: Low-beta stocks often yield higher returns than predicted.
            - Multifactor Shifts: Replaced or augmented by Fama-French 3/5 factor models.

8. Evaluating performance

    - Risk-adjusted measures - sharp ratio, Treynor measure, Jensen's alpha information ratio, M^2, Morningstar rating
    - Performance attribution - how much of the result came from asset allocation, stock pricing and market timing
    - Style analysis - inferring a fund's real strategy from its returns, and checking whether it matches what the fund claims
    - Hedge funds = hard to assess because their strategies = opaque, unconventional, and extreme in risk. So, standard measures don't work well

Glossary:
1. Active management:
Adjusting a portfolio's holdings to try to beat the market, through stock picking or market timing

2. Algorithmic trading:
Trading carried out automatically by computer programs following set rules

3. Arbitrage:
Profiting from price differences by buying the cheap asset and selling the expensive one, which pushes prices back into line

4. Asset allocation:
Deciding how to split money between broad classes such as bonds, shares and cash. Strategic allocation is the long-term plan; tactical allocation is short-term adjustment

5. Behavioural finance:
Field that uses psychology to explain why investors sometimes act irrationally

6. Benchmark / index:
A reference portfolio, such as the FTSE 100, used to judge a fund's performance

7. Black–Litterman model
Model that combines CAPM's market-based return estimates with a manager's own views

8. Bond:
A loan to a government or company that pays interest; risk depends on the borrower

9. CAPM (Capital Asset Pricing Model):
Theory linking an asset's expected return to its exposure to overall market risk

10. Certificate of deposit
A low-risk bank deposit held for a fixed term

11. Commodities:
Raw materials such as oil, gold or wheat traded as investments

12. Credit rating:
A score of how likely a borrower is to repay; it drives bond risk

13. Derivative:
A contract whose value depends on another asset, such as an option or future

14. Diversification:
Spreading investments across many assets to reduce risk

15. Efficient markets hypothesis (EMH):
The idea that prices already reflect all available information, so you can't consistently beat the market

16. Equity:
Shares which menas you have part-ownership of a company with a claim on profits left after creditors are paid

17. ETF (exchange-traded fund):
A pooled fund that trades on a stock exchange like a share

18. Financial intermediary:
A go-between connecting savers and borrowers, such as a bank, broker or fund

19. Fundamental analysis:
Valuing a security from company financials and economic data to find mispricing

20. Hedge fund:
A lightly regulated fund using flexible, often high-risk strategies

21. High-frequency trading:
Very fast algorithmic trading that holds positions for fractions of a second

22. Information ratio:
A fund's excess return over its benchmark divided by the variability of that excess

23. IPO (initial public offering):
A private company selling shares to the public for the first time
Jensen's alpha	The return a fund earns above what CAPM predicts for its level of risk

24. Leverage:
Using borrowed money to increase the size of an investment, which magnifies both gains and losses

25. Limits to arbitrage:
Reasons such as cost, risk and capital constraints that stop traders from correcting mispricings

26. Liquidity:
How easily an asset can be sold without affecting its price
Margin (buying on)	Buying securities partly with money borrowed from a broker

27. Market timing:
Shifting a portfolio in anticipation of market moves

28. Markowitz portfolio theory:
Framework for building the best risk-return portfolio through diversification

29. Money market:
Market for short-term, very low-risk debt such as Treasury bills

30. Mutual fund:
A pooled investment fund managed by professionals on behalf of many investors

31. Passive management:
Holding fixed target weights, or tracking an index, rather than trying to beat the market

32. Pension fund:
A pool of investments set aside to pay members' retirement income
Performance attribution	Breaking a fund's returns into what came from allocation, selection and timing

33. Primary market:
Where newly issued securities are first sold

34. Risk aversion:
Dislike of uncertainty; a risk-averse investor needs higher returns to accept more risk

35. Risk-free asset:
An investment with near-certain returns, such as a Treasury bill

36. Seasoned equity offering:
An already-listed company issuing additional shares

37. Secondary market:
Where existing securities are traded between investors

38. Sharpe ratio:
Excess return over the risk-free rate divided by standard deviation; return per unit of total risk

39. Short selling:
Selling borrowed shares in the hope of buying them back cheaper later

40. Standard deviation / variance:
Measures of how spread out returns are; the module's measure of risk

41. Style analysis:
Inferring a fund's investment style by comparing its returns with various indices

42. Technical analysis:
Predicting prices from patterns in past price and volume charts

43. Treasury bill:
A short-term government debt instrument, treated as essentially risk-free

44. Treynor measure:
Excess return divided by beta, which measures market risk only

45. Treynor–Black model:
Simplified approach that relates each stock to a market index, reducing the estimates needed for optimisation

46. Unit investment trust:
A fixed, unmanaged portfolio of securities sold in units to investors
